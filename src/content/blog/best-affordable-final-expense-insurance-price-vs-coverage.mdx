"Affordable" and "best coverage" get treated like they're in tension with each other, as if you have to sacrifice one for the other. Sometimes that's true. Often, it's not — it just requires understanding the actual tradeoff instead of guessing at it. Here's how to think through price versus coverage without shortchanging either one.

## Why this feels like a tradeoff in the first place

More coverage costs more, which is straightforward enough. But "affordable" isn't really about the smallest possible premium — it's about a premium that fits your budget attached to coverage that actually does what you need it to. Those two things aren't automatically at odds; they just require a clearer-eyed comparison than "which number is lowest."

## The real question: affordable for what?

Before comparing price against coverage, it helps to get specific about what you're actually trying to accomplish:

- **Just funeral costs?** A smaller coverage amount, like $10,000, might genuinely be both affordable and sufficient.
- **Funeral costs plus some cushion?** $15,000 often strikes a real balance here.
- **Funeral costs plus outstanding debts?** $20,000 or more may be necessary regardless of the premium, since underinsuring doesn't actually solve the problem it's meant to.

## A framework for balancing the two

**Start with the minimum coverage that actually accomplishes your goal.** Don't default to the largest tier out of general caution if a smaller amount genuinely covers what you need.

**Compare simplified issue and guaranteed issue if you're not sure which applies.** Simplified issue is often more affordable if you qualify, so it's worth checking rather than assuming you need guaranteed issue.

**Look at the premium jump between tiers, not just the sticker price of each.** Sometimes moving from $10,000 to $15,000 costs less than expected, making the larger amount the better value. Sometimes the jump is substantial enough that the smaller amount is clearly the smarter choice.

**Factor in your actual monthly budget, not a hypothetical one.** A policy that's technically affordable on paper but genuinely tight against your real monthly expenses isn't a good long-term fit, even if the coverage amount looks right.

## A worked example

Imagine you're deciding between $10,000 and $15,000 in coverage. If the premium difference between them is small — say, $8 a month — the extra coverage is likely worth it for most people, since $8 rarely changes someone's monthly budget meaningfully. But if that gap is $25 or $30 a month, it's worth asking honestly whether the extra coverage is solving a real problem or just providing a level of cushion you don't actually need. There's no universal right answer — the point is running this comparison with real numbers instead of assuming either direction.

## Where people commonly get this wrong

**Assuming cheap always means underinsured.** Plenty of genuinely affordable policies provide real, adequate coverage — "cheap" and "insufficient" aren't the same thing.

**Assuming more expensive always means better protected.** Sometimes a higher premium just reflects a larger coverage amount than you need, not meaningfully better terms.

**Comparing premiums without comparing what's actually covered.** A slightly cheaper guaranteed issue quote and a slightly more expensive simplified issue quote for the same coverage amount aren't equivalent products — the terms differ meaningfully.

## The hidden cost of underinsuring to save a few dollars

It's worth being direct about the other side of this tradeoff too: choosing the cheapest possible option by underinsuring doesn't actually save money in any real sense — it just shifts the shortfall onto your family at the exact moment they're least equipped to absorb it. A $5,000 policy that saves $15 a month compared to a $10,000 policy isn't a bargain if it leaves your family covering a $5,000 gap out of pocket during an already difficult time. "Affordable" has to be evaluated against what actually gets covered, not just against the smallest monthly number available.

## How to actually shop with this framework

1. **Decide the minimum coverage amount that accomplishes your actual goal.**
2. **Get quotes for that amount and one tier above it**, to see the real price difference.
3. **Compare policy types** if you're not sure which one you'd qualify for.
4. **Choose based on genuine fit** — not the lowest number, and not the largest number, but the one that balances both sides of the equation for your specific situation.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is there a rule of thumb for how much of my budget final expense insurance should take up?**
There's no universal percentage — the right amount is whatever premium you can comfortably sustain for as long as you'll be paying it, compared against a coverage amount that actually meets your goals.

**Does choosing a cheaper option always mean sacrificing something important?**
Not necessarily — sometimes a lower-priced option genuinely provides adequate coverage for your specific situation, especially if you don't need the largest available amount.

**How do I know if I'm overpaying for more coverage than I need?**
Comparing the actual premium difference between coverage tiers, rather than assuming a bigger number is automatically better, is the clearest way to check.

**Is it worth revisiting this balance if my financial situation changes?**
If your budget or needs change significantly, it's reasonable to compare your options again, though changing existing coverage generally means applying for a new policy priced at your current age.

**What's the fastest way to see this tradeoff in real numbers?**
Getting quotes for a couple of coverage amounts side by side shows you the actual price gap far more reliably than estimating it in the abstract.

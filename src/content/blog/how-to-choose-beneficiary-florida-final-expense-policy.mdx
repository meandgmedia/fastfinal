Choosing a beneficiary sounds like it should be simple — just pick someone you trust. In practice, a few details trip people up, and getting them right matters more than most applicants realize until it's too late to fix.

## What a beneficiary actually does

Your beneficiary is the person (or people) who receives the death benefit from your final expense policy. They're the one who files the claim and receives the cash payout, which they can then use for funeral costs, other expenses, or however else makes sense.

## Who can be a beneficiary

Most people name a spouse, adult child, or other close family member. Some people name a trusted friend, especially if they don't have close family, or split the benefit among multiple people. There's no requirement that your beneficiary be a blood relative — the choice is entirely yours.

## Common approaches people take

**Naming a spouse or partner.** The most common choice, especially when that person would naturally be the one handling arrangements.

**Naming an adult child.** Common when there's no spouse, or when a specific child is expected to take on funeral planning responsibilities.

**Splitting the benefit among multiple people.** Some policies allow you to name multiple beneficiaries with specified percentages, useful if you want to divide responsibility or ensure fairness among children.

**Naming a funeral home or trust directly.** Less common, but possible in some cases, depending on the specific policy and your goals.

## Mistakes worth avoiding

**Never updating your beneficiary after a major life change.** Divorce, remarriage, a beneficiary passing away before you — all of these are reasons to review and potentially update your designation, and it's easy to forget this after the initial application.

**Not telling your beneficiary the policy exists.** A policy your beneficiary doesn't know about can't be claimed promptly. Make sure whoever you name actually knows the coverage exists and roughly where to find the details.

**Naming someone without discussing it with them.** If you're expecting a specific person to handle funeral arrangements using this benefit, it's worth having that conversation directly rather than assuming they'll know what to do.

**Leaving the designation blank or unclear.** This can complicate and delay the claims process — take the time to name a specific person clearly.

## Can you name more than one beneficiary?

Often, yes. Many final expense policies allow you to name multiple beneficiaries and specify how the benefit should be divided between them — commonly by percentage. This can be useful if you want to share responsibility among adult children, for example, rather than placing it entirely on one person.

## Primary versus contingent beneficiaries, explained simply

Your primary beneficiary is who receives the benefit under normal circumstances. A contingent beneficiary is a backup — someone who receives the benefit only if your primary beneficiary is no longer living or otherwise unable to receive it. Naming both isn't required, but it's a simple, low-effort way to make sure the benefit still goes exactly where you intend even if circumstances change unexpectedly between when you apply and when the policy is eventually needed.

## What happens if your beneficiary passes away before you

This is exactly why many policies allow you to name a contingent (backup) beneficiary — someone who receives the benefit if your primary beneficiary is no longer living when the time comes. If you haven't named a contingent beneficiary and your primary beneficiary has passed away, the benefit may go through a different process depending on your policy's specific terms, which is part of why keeping this updated matters.

## How often should you review your beneficiary designation

There's no fixed schedule, but reviewing it after any major life event — marriage, divorce, the birth of children or grandchildren, or the death of a previously named beneficiary — is a reasonable practice. A quick review every few years, even without a specific triggering event, is also a reasonable habit.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I change my beneficiary after the policy is already in place?**
Yes — most policies allow you to update your beneficiary designation at any time, typically through a simple form with your carrier.

**Do I need a lawyer to name a beneficiary?**
No — this is typically a straightforward part of the application or a simple update form, not something requiring legal assistance.

**What if I want my final expense benefit to go through my estate instead of a named person?**
This is possible in some cases but generally more complicated and slower than naming a specific beneficiary directly — most people prefer naming an individual for simplicity and speed.

**Can a minor be named as a beneficiary?**
This varies by policy and often involves additional considerations, such as a guardian managing funds until the minor reaches adulthood — worth discussing directly with your carrier if this applies to you.

**Is my beneficiary designation private?**
Yes — this information is between you and your insurance carrier, not publicly disclosed.
